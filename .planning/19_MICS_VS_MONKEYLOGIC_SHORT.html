MICS vs MonkeyLogic

Everything that matters, stripped to the conclusions. The full version has the evidence.

01The two systems in one line each

MonkeyLogic

One program, one PC, one rig, one scientist watching. Free, 18 years mature, and close to ideal for a frame-locked visual task aligned to neural recording.

MICS

Many rigs on a shared database, tasks built in a browser without code, running unattended for months — and built to plug other systems in.

02What Yoav actually does

A mouse held at 80% body weight — being thirsty is the thing studied. Head fixed under a microscope photographing brain cells 31 times a second. A screen shows moving stripes whose tilt is the message: one angle means water, another means salt. 2 s of stripes, 2 s to lick, first lick only. Trained means >80% right on the good cue and <50% on the bad one.

Bottom line

MonkeyLogic draws the stripes exactly on time, watches the licks, writes one page per trial, and sends pulses so the brain images line up. His experiment is close to the exact thing MonkeyLogic was built for. Don't try to beat it there.

03The differences that matter

TopicBottom line
Clocks A MonkeyLogic rig does not have one clock — it has four or five, and they disagree. Events are timed by the PC's clock. Analog signals are timed by the DAQ board's own crystal. Anything without a clock of its own — including all digital inputs, the parallel port, touchscreens and network eye trackers — is simply checked on a repeating timer at roughly 1 ms, ± 0.22 ms. The screen adds its own refresh cycle, and a second DAQ board adds a fifth.

NIMH states the consequence plainly: the PC clock and the board clock drift apart, "which results in a missing/extra sample every few minutes" — and ships an offline correction step to patch it up afterwards. Their own advice is to feed every signal into the neural recording system instead, so that at least it digitises everything on one clock.

What that means in practice: when he asks "when did the mouse lick, relative to this brain image?", the lick time and the image sync came from different clocks, and the answer has to be reconstructed after the session.

MICS has one clock. Every digital line on the rig — licks, beam breaks, sync pulses — is timestamped by dedicated hardware in microseconds, without waiting for the software to notice. There is nothing to line up afterwards because there was never a second clock.
use this Licks are digital. Theirs are timed by a software timer at about a millisecond; ours in microseconds, on the same clock as everything else.
but concede Knowing when something happened is not the same as reacting quickly. Our measured response loop is 5.2 ms on average and 46 ms at worst — measured before the latency fixes and never repeated.
The CPU Neither system trusts its own CPU; both push timing onto dedicated hardware — theirs a preloaded DAQ board, ours the GPIO chip and precompiled pulse scripts.
use this "It's Python, therefore imprecise" is not a valid argument — our critical paths sit below Python exactly as theirs sit below MATLAB.
Writing to a file MonkeyLogic writes one file per session, onto the hard disk of that rig's own PC. The file is excellent as far as it goes: one record per trial, the same guaranteed fields in every task ever written, and it is written between trials so saving can never disturb timing.

But being a file on one machine has four consequences:
• Nobody else can see it while it runs. The only live view is the screen on that rig.
• Comparing across sessions is manual. Their summary tool accepts exactly one filename. Nine mice over three months means opening ~200 files by hand, or writing your own MATLAB to do it.
• It is one copy, in one place. Backup is whatever the lab remembers to do.
• The task itself is not in it. The script is compiled when it loads and its text is never written down — so a file from 2022 does not tell you what the task actually did.

MICS writes every event and every state change into a shared database. Anyone in the lab can open a live dashboard from their own desk while the session is running, and questions spanning animals and months are a query rather than a project. The task is stored and content-hashed alongside the data.
but concede Their trial record is guaranteed by the format. Ours is recorded by convention — names and shapes vary between tasks. Fixing that is agreeing on names, not building new machinery.
Analog The one real gap. They sample 16 channels at 1 kHz always. We have none.
concede flatly Then note the fix is a board plus a driver, not a redesign.
Boundary They do everything themselves — "NIMH ML does not collect data automatically in the background. You should handle everything yourself." We plug things in, and outside signals become ordinary named values usable in the task builder.
use this Demonstrated: a Mac streamed live paw coordinates into a running task on a rig at 60 Hz for two minutes.
but concede Latency was never measured. Quote the 60 Hz, not milliseconds.

05Weak spots in his current setup

  • His task code is not in his data. The script is compiled at load and never stored — a 2022 file doesn't tell you what the task did. The clearest place we are ahead.
  • Several clocks that must be reconciled after the fact.
  • Licks are timed by software, ~1 ms, not by hardware.
  • Screen delay is his to measure — one user found 50.9 ms where the published figure is ~0. If unmeasured, his cue onset may be tens of ms off relative to imaging.
  • Dropped frames — and his stimulus is the vulnerable case. The screen redraws 60 times a second, and the software must have the next picture ready before each redraw. If it is even slightly late the frame is skipped: the stripes stutter, the drift speed is briefly wrong, and the moment the mouse actually saw the cue shifts. This is a well-populated complaint on their own support forum — one user reported 30 skipped frames in every trial on video stimuli — and the official troubleshooting goes as far as editing Windows graphics registry keys. Moving gratings are precisely the case that suffers.
  • No colony database. Weights and restriction schedules live in spreadsheets — for a lab whose variable is deprivation state.
  • One rig needs one whole PC. Two copies of MonkeyLogic will not run on one machine — the maintainer's words: "Unfortunately it does not work in that way, mostly because of the graphics." So eight rigs means eight Windows PCs, each with two monitors and its own DAQ board, each administered, updated and licensed separately. Scaling costs floor space and maintenance, not just money. And there is no remote control at all — the maintainer's suggestion for monitoring from elsewhere is "long cables", or a notification to your phone.
  • Nothing restarts it if it falls over. There is no autostart, no watchdog and no crash recovery. If a session dies at 2 a.m., it stays dead until somebody walks in. The distribution ships a utility for repairing corrupted data files, which tells you corruption happens in practice. Searching their forum for "unattended" returns nothing, because running unattended was never a goal — the published home-cage study using MonkeyLogic ran about 90 minutes a day with staff moving the animals.
  • One maintainer, no public repository — the fairest reciprocal question he can ask us.

06Never say these

Both are false and would cost you the room

"MATLAB licensing makes it expensive to scale." ~$550 academic, often free under a campus agreement — the DAQ board costs four times more.

"MonkeyLogic can't do closed loop." It has shipped a working example since November 2017.

07Four answers to have ready

"How would this help my science?"
Not by training his mice — he trains in MonkeyLogic too. By instrumenting the weeks around the rig: weight curves, surgeries, protocols, and questions across animals and days. Ask how they track restriction today.
"Can you show my visual stimulus?"
Not today — but the code exists unused, and it's install-and-calibrate, not a redesign.
"Do you handle analog?"
No. Then: board, driver, batching — and ask which of his signals genuinely need analog, since the frame clock, capacitive licking and a running wheel are all digital.
"What's your lick-to-reward latency?"
Never measured. Say so, then say what you'd measure and how. That lands better than a number he can't check.

The line that ties it together: they are ahead where two decades on one problem compound — the eye, the visual primitives, the installed base. We are ahead where the architecture is younger — one clock across every line, tasks as data rather than code, and everything above the single session. Most of our gap is a short list of known work; most of theirs is time.
