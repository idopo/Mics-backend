02 — The constraint

Why we count pins

The Raspberry Pi exposes a single 40-pin connector. After power and ground, 28 of those pins can carry signals, and every one of them is currently spoken for. That is the budget this whole plan works within.

The good news is that most add-on hardware does not consume pins. Two of the 28 form a shared communication bus called I2C, and any number of small boards can hang off those same two wires — each one answering to its own address, like houses on a street. A board on the I2C bus is effectively free.

Only three kinds of thing genuinely cost pins: signals that must be timed to the microsecond, the audio connection (which the chip fixes to three specific pins), and interrupt lines — a single wire a sensor uses to say “something just happened, read me now.”

The rule of thumb for the rest of this document: if a question is “can we add another one of these?”, the answer is usually yes and it costs no pins. If the question is “does this need to be timed precisely?”, that is when it costs a pin.

Function by function

Each block below gives what the function is for, what exists today, and the realistic options. The shaded row is our recommendation.

Light intensity

Function 02 · new

Setting how bright the optogenetic LED runs — a steady voltage the light driver reads as a brightness command.

Today: nothing. The Pi cannot produce a variable voltage on any pin. This capability requires an add-on board whichever route we take.
OptionCostPinsTrade-off
Voltage-output board on the shared bus recommendedMCP4728 — 4-channel analog voltage output€50Four independent brightness channels, updated every ~0.2 ms. Ample for setting a level, a ramp, or the envelope of a pulse train.
Filtered switching from a pinhardware PWM + RC filter€11No extra chip, but coarser (~8–10 bits) and slower to settle. Costs a pin we do not have spare.

Design point worth agreeing now: the voltage board sets how bright; a separate signal pin sets when. Set the brightness during the inter-trial interval, then gate the light with a precisely-timed pulse. This is exactly how commercial optogenetic drivers are built, and it keeps stimulus onset accurate to microseconds.

Treadmill

Function 03 · new

Reading locomotion speed. The sensor sends a voltage that rises with speed, on a two-conductor connection.

Today: nothing. The Pi cannot measure a voltage on any pin, so this also needs an add-on board.
OptionCostPinsTrade-off
Voltage-reader board on the shared bus recommendedADS1115 — 4-channel analog voltage reader€50One reading every ~1.2 ms, against locomotion that changes over 10–100 ms. Four inputs, so three spare for future sensors.
Fast reader on a separate busMCP3008 — 8-channel SPI ADC€54~50× faster than we need for this, and costs four pins. Not justified.
Two things to check before wiring, both cheap: measure the sensor's voltage at full speed, so the protection circuit is sized correctly — these boards have hard limits and a treadmill can exceed them. And note that a two-conductor sensor reports speed but not direction. If forward-versus-backward ever matters scientifically, that is a different sensor, not a different board.

Doors and motors

Function 04

Opening and closing compartment doors, and the motorised reward mechanism.

Today: an Adafruit motor board stacked on top of the Pi's connector. Internally it is two ordinary chips — a signal generator and the power stage that actually turns the motors. It uses the shared bus and no pins at all.

That draws maximum current indefinitely and will eventually destroy the motor or its driver. Two switches per door, on a shared expander board, cost €5 and no pins.

Optogenetic stimulator link

Function 06

Commanding the head-mounted wireless stimulator to deliver light.

Today: a signal wire from the Pi to a separate control unit, which relays the command wirelessly. The wire costs one pin.
OptionCostPinsTrade-off
Keep the signal wireBNC trigger to the BlueHub€01Stimulation starts within microseconds of the command. Required if onset must be locked to behaviour.
Pi talks to the stimulator directly over BluetoothBluetooth Low Energy, onboard€0−1Frees a pin. But stimulation onset becomes uncertain by 7–30 ms, because of how Bluetooth schedules messages.
This is a scientific decision, not a technical one, and it changes the pin budget. If your protocols tolerate tens of milliseconds of onset uncertainty, Bluetooth frees the pin the audio chip needs. If onset must be locked to a lick or a poke, the wire stays and the audio chip's third pin has to come from elsewhere.

Either way the rig still needs the existing control unit for charging and programming the wearables — Bluetooth cannot do either.

Valves, LEDs and beam breaks

Function 07

The ordinary on/off outputs and sensing inputs a task needs — reward valves, odour valves, cue lights, infrared beam breaks.

Today: 22 of these, each on its own pin. That is the bulk of the budget, and it is why nothing else fits.
OptionCostPinsTrade-off
Keep the precise ones on pins recommended€01 eachAnything whose exact timing appears in the analysis — reward delivery, beam breaks that gate a trial.

The question that sorts them: does this line's exact timing appear in your analysis? If yes it stays on a pin. If no, it moves to the expander and costs nothing. This is a scientific judgement per line, and it is the single most useful thing to settle before the board is laid out.
04 — How it all connects

The whole rig on one page

Three ways a device can reach the Pi: it takes its own pins, it shares the two-wire bus with everything else, or it needs no wires at all. The dashed lines are the subject of section 06.

05 — The shared bus

One bus, one address per board

Every add-on board above shares the same two wires. Each answers to its own address, so the only planning needed is to make sure no two want the same one.

AddressBoardFunctionMax on the bus
0x20–0x27MCP2301716 extra on/off lines8
0x40–0x47PCA9685motor and servo signals8
0x48–0x4BADS1115read a voltage — treadmill4
0x5A–0x5DMPR121lick detection4
0x60–0x67MCP4728set a voltage — light intensity8
One decision to record now: the light-intensity board and the stacked motor board both default to address 0x60. Buying the motor chips separately avoids this entirely, because a standalone signal generator sits at 0x40. If we keep the stacked board instead, it needs one solder link to move it to 0x61.

A realistic rig is five boards against a 112-address space. Neither addresses nor bus speed are anywhere near a limit — the practical ceiling is wiring length, and the fix for that is a signal buffer, not a second bus.
06 — Timing

Knowing exactly when something happened

A signal on its own pin is timestamped by the Pi's hardware to within a millionth of a second. A message arriving over the shared bus is not — there we only know when the software got round to reading it, which can be a thousandth of a second out, and varies.

For a brightness setting that does not matter. For “when did the door actually open” or “when was the treadmill at this speed”, it does.

The fix is one extra wire per board. Most of these chips have a spare pin that pulses at the exact moment something happens; run that to a Pi pin and the event gets the hardware timestamp, while the bus carries the detail. The rig already works this way for licks — the sensor's alert wire says when, and the bus read afterwards says which electrode.

Witness wireComes fromRecords the moment…
Lick already fittedMPR121an electrode is touched
Door arrivalMCP23017a limit switch closes — the door physically arriving
TreadmillADS1115a speed reading completes
Light changeMCP4728the brightness actually changes
The door case is the clearest. The command we send over the bus was never the door's arrival time — there is mechanical travel in between. The limit switch closing is the arrival. Recording both gives us the travel time, measured, on every trial.

The treadmill does not need every sample timestamped. We take one hardware-timed anchor every hundred readings and fill in between, which also measures the sensor board's own clock drifting against the rig's. Same technique, far less load.
These four wires cost four pins, and that is what forces one design change. The budget balances at zero without them. Moving the five odour valves onto the expander board pays for all four — and odour is the right thing to move, because odour arrival is limited by how fast gas travels down a tube, not by electronics. This is no longer an optional refinement; it is part of the plan.

The rig needs 21 of those last 17. Four short — resolved by moving the five odour valves onto the expander board, which leaves 16 needed against 17 available: one spare. One line short, which is resolved by moving a single timing-tolerant output — an odour valve, say — onto the expander board. That is a €3 part and no pins.

It is worth stating plainly because it is the whole shape of the problem: the connector is full, and every new capability has to be paid for either by a board on the shared bus, or by moving something slow off a pin.

10 — Decisions

What we need agreed

Four of these are scientific judgements and cannot be made by the hardware side. The rest are bench checks.

01
Can optogenetic stimulation tolerate 7–30 ms of onset uncertainty?
If yes, Bluetooth replaces the trigger wire and frees the pin the audio chip needs. If no, the wire stays and one slow output moves to an expander instead. This is the single decision that determines the pin layout.
Scientific — PI
02
Which control lines must be timed precisely?
Line by line: does its exact timing appear in the analysis? Everything that does stays on a pin; everything that does not moves to an expander for free. This sets the whole layout.
Scientific — PI
03
Is the current sound quality actually a problem, or only its timing?
The timing fault is a software bug and the fix is free. If the cue is a simple beep, the built-in output may be sufficient and we save three pins. If stimuli need calibrated intensity, the audio chip is required.
Scientific — PI
04
Could the doors be servo-driven rather than motor-driven?
A four-fold reduction in board count and an eight-fold reduction in cost at scale. Depends entirely on whether the mechanism allows it.
Mechanical — engineering
05
Measure the treadmill sensor's voltage at full speed.
Two minutes with a multimeter, and the protection circuit design follows from that one number.
Bench check
06
Confirm the light driver's control-input specification.
If it accepts 0–3.3 V it connects directly with no additional circuitry at all. If it wants 0–5 V, a small amplifier stage is needed.
Bench check
07
Confirm the stacked motor board is not claiming two pins.
A one-line check on the rig. If it is, the audio plan needs those pins back and the motor board has to come off the connector regardless.
