MICS vs MonkeyLogic

What NIMH MonkeyLogic is, how the Livneh lab uses it, where MICS genuinely differs — and what MICS would need to change to serve, or improve on, that setup.

Read this first

MonkeyLogic is excellent at what it was built for. It is free, actively maintained, 18 years mature, and close to ideal for a frame-locked visual task aligned to neural recording. Say that first and mean it.

The two systems answer different questions. MonkeyLogic gives unlimited freedom inside one session, because a task is code. MICS gives unlimited memory across sessions, because a task is data — and it is built to plug other systems in. Each pays for the other's strength.

Section 01What MonkeyLogic is

A free, MATLAB-based system for running behavioural experiments and collecting the data. You write your trial as a plain MATLAB script — in the same language you already use for analysis — and it runs with tightly controlled screen timing, 1 kHz sampling of analog inputs, and sub-millisecond event pulses to a neural recording system.

In plain words

It is one program, on one Windows PC, driving one rig, with one scientist watching. It shows things on a screen exactly on time, watches what the animal does, gives rewards, and writes a diary with one page per trial.

What it is genuinely best-in-class at

  • Screen timing you can prove. It ships its own measurement kit — a latency test, a photodiode tuner, and 10 benchmark tasks. Very few systems do.
  • Eye tracking as a first-class citizen — five vendors, two calibration methods, drift correction, proper visual-angle coordinates.
  • Neural alignment via strobed event codes with a configurable strobe spec.
  • A deep vocabulary of primate-psychophysics building blocks — 50+ ready-made behavioural components accumulated over two decades.
  • Trial replay — it can reconstruct and re-play any trial as video.
Their own honest caveat

NIMH says it plainly: "MonkeyLogic is not a true real-time system like DOS Cortex, it is fast enough for most behavioral work…" — and the download page tells every user to measure timing on their own hardware, because it depends on the machine.

Section 02How the Livneh lab uses it — explained simply

Verified from their own published Methods (Cell Rep 2024): "Behavioral training was performed using MonkeyLogic and MonkeyLogic2." So it runs both the training and the experiment.

The experiment, in plain words

A mouse is kept a little thirsty — held at about 80% of its normal body weight — because being thirsty is the thing being studied.

Its head is held still under a microscope that photographs living brain cells 31 times a second, looking sideways into a deep brain area through a tiny glass prism.

A screen in front of the mouse shows moving stripes. The tilt of the stripes is the message: one angle means water is coming, another means something nasty is coming, a third means nothing.

The stripes show for 2 seconds. Then the mouse has 2 seconds to lick. Only the first lick counts. Lick after the good stripes and it gets a tiny drop of water; lick after the bad stripes and it gets salt water.

The mouse counts as trained when it gets the good cue right more than 80% of the time and licks the bad cue less than half the time.

MonkeyLogic's job in that experiment

  1. Draw the moving stripes exactly on the beat — the pattern is only correct if every single screen frame arrives on time.
  2. Watch the licks and open the water valve on the first one.
  3. Write one page per trial — which cue, did it lick, right or wrong, how fast.
  4. Send pulses so the brain pictures and the behaviour line up afterwards.
Why this suits MonkeyLogic almost perfectly

His cue is a moving picture, not a light. That needs a screen redrawn dozens of times a second, in degrees of the animal's visual field — which is MonkeyLogic's core competence, with ready-made grating objects and the visual-angle maths built in.

His analysis is trial-shaped, and MonkeyLogic hands him trials already assembled. And it is MATLAB — the same language as the rest of his pipeline.

His experiment is close to the exact thing MonkeyLogic was designed for. Do not go looking for a way to beat it here.

Section 03Where the two systems really differ

Two things drive everything else. In MonkeyLogic a task is code you write; in MICS it is data you assemble. And MonkeyLogic is one program on one computer, while MICS is a network that plugs other systems in.

3.1 · Clocks

A MonkeyLogic rig runs on several clocks that have to be lined up afterwards. A MICS rig runs on one.

NIMH is candid about the first pair: "ML2 uses the CPU clock to time events, while analog signals are digitized based on the NI board's clock… which results in a missing/extra sample every few minutes." The fix is an offline correction step.

There is a third clock that is easy to miss and matters most here. Anything without a clock of its own — digital inputs, the parallel port, touchscreens, USB joysticks, network eye trackers — is simply checked on a timer, at about 1 ms, ± 0.22 ms. Add the screen's own refresh, a second board if you need two stimulation channels, and an Arduino if reward runs through one.

MICS puts every digital line on one clock. Signal changes are timestamped by dedicated hardware in microseconds, without waiting for the software to notice. Nothing needs lining up afterwards, because there is only one clock to begin with.

The point — and the half that must go with it

The famous 1 kHz is the analog path. Digital inputs — licks, beam breaks, levers, which is most behaviour — are checked by a software timer at roughly a millisecond. MICS timestamps those same events in hardware, a thousand times finer.

But knowing when is not the same as reacting fast. MICS's measured response loop is 5.2 ms on average, 46 ms at worst — and that was measured before the latency fixes and never repeated. MICS knows when things happened far better than it can promise how quickly it answers.

3.2 · Why neither system trusts its own computer

Ordinary computers are bad at being punctual — the operating system does its own housekeeping whenever it likes. Both systems solve this the same way: move the timing-critical work off the CPU and onto dedicated hardware.

MonkeyLogic → the DAQ board

Precise output works because the waveform is loaded into the board in advance and the board plays it; the PC only pulls the trigger. Screen timing works by waiting for the display's refresh, with missed frames counted.

"As a general purpose machine, a Windows PC cannot provide that kind of timing accuracy that stimulation output requires." — maintainer

Where the CPU still shows through: dropped frames are a recurring complaint — one user reported 30 per trial on video stimuli.

MICS → the GPIO chip

Timestamps are taken by dedicated hardware, not by Python. Precise output pulses are compiled in advance into a small program the GPIO daemon runs — the same trick as loading a board buffer.

So "it's Python, therefore imprecise" doesn't follow. The critical parts already sit below Python, just as MonkeyLogic's sit below MATLAB.

Where the CPU still shows through: the decide-and-respond path, with the latency tail above.

3.3 · One file per session vs. a shared database

MonkeyLogic writes a diary with one page per trial, into a file on that rig's computer. MICS writes every event into a database the whole lab can read, from anywhere, while the experiment is still running.

Both approaches have a real advantage. MonkeyLogic's trial record is guaranteed — every task ever written produces the same fields in the same documented format — and it writes to disk between trials, so saving can never disturb trial timing. MICS records the whole trajectory instead: a live query returns 2.9 million events, 930,000 of them state changes, alongside explicit hit, miss and false-alarm records. So the route through a trial is recoverable, not just how it ended.

The trade

MonkeyLogic guarantees the trial and loses the stream; MICS captures the stream and loses the guarantee. Our fix is agreeing on consistent names, not building a trial table.

And note the reversal on provenance: the system with the looser data format keeps the better record of what was actually run.

3.4 · Analog signals — the one real gap

Some signals aren't "something happened" — they're a value that's always there, wobbling: eye position, pupil size, force, a voltage from a neural rig. There's no moment to react to. You have to look at regular intervals, forever.

MonkeyLogic does this on 16 channels, a thousand times a second, always. MICS cannot do it at all — there is no analog input anywhere in the system. Concede this flatly. Two things a regular grid gives you that an event stream cannot:

  1. A continuously varying signal has no edge to trigger on. Without a grid it isn't in the record at all — not at poor resolution, at none.
  2. A grid tells you when nothing happened. In an event stream, silence could mean nothing occurred, the detector missed it, or the record was dropped. A guaranteed sample per interval separates those.

The gap is real but shallow: the fix is a board and a driver, not a redesign — see Section 4.

3.5 · MICS plugs things in; MonkeyLogic mostly doesn't

MonkeyLogic is one program that does everything itself. MICS is a switchboard — its answer to "can you do X?" is usually "we plug X in and keep it on our clock."

MonkeyLogic marks the boundary explicitly: "NIMH ML does not collect data automatically in the background. You should handle everything yourself." MICS is built the other way round. Outside devices connect over a typed link that understands when a source has gone quiet, and — the important part — their signals become ordinary named values in the task builder, usable in a decision exactly like a lick.

So capability questions turn into integration questions. The pattern is always the same two parts: a pulse sets the time, a link carries the values.

Already demonstrated

A driver on a separate machine has streamed live values — left_paw_x, right_paw_x, and a structured object_detected event — into a running task on a rig over the network, sustained at 60 Hz for two minutes, behaving correctly when the source went quiet: one signal decaying to a default, another holding its last value. That is a pose-tracking stream driving a live experiment.

Limits: the demonstrated direction is the device connecting inward; and round-trip latency was deliberately never measured. Quote the 60 Hz, not a millisecond figure.

Section 04What MICS would need to change

Two lists: what it would take to serve a setup like his at all, and where MICS would then improve on it. Effort is shown on a five-point scale.

built in  ·  config  ·  write a module  ·  buy hardware  ·  architectural

4.1 · To serve his kind of experiment

ChangeEffortWhy it matters here
Accuracy-based graduation His training rule is ">80% right on the good cue AND <50% on the bad one". MICS today can only say "do N trials". This is roughly 50 lines plus a form field — the cheapest high-value fix in the system, and it is literally his criterion.
Visual stimuli A complete moving-grating implementation already sits in the codebase, unused — right parameters, own thread, waits for the screen's beat. What is missing is installing the graphics stack, calibrating degrees-of-visual-angle, and giving it a vocabulary in the task builder. Days-to-weeks, not architecture.
Analog input Add an ADC board. The clever part is keeping one clock — see below.
Analog output For commanding laser intensity. MICS has no DAC; it does already have hardware-timed digital pulse trains, which is how much optogenetics is actually gated.
Microscope sync Exchange pulses with the imaging frame clock. The pulse hardware exists; the wiring in the live toolkit is currently switched off. One config change plus one demo run.
Standardise trial & outcome events Turns data already being collected into a guarantee: one spelling, one payload shape, a trial number on every record.
Trial-shaped live plots Not a gap — MICS already has live dashboards over the live data via Kibana, viewable remotely and built per cohort. What his control screen adds is plots bound to trial structure out of the box. Once trial events are standardised (above), the same dashboards give it.
Keeping one clock when you add an ADC — in plain words

The measuring board has its own wristwatch, and it runs slightly fast. Instead of arguing with its watch, you make it tap you on the shoulder every time it takes a measurement — and you write down the time, on your clock.

That tap is a real wire, into a pin, stamped in microseconds by the same clock that stamps every lick. So the board's drift stops being an error and becomes a measurement.

This is better than MonkeyLogic's position, not merely equal to it — they assume the board's clock is regular and discover the discrepancy afterwards. Nothing needs reconciling if you wrote down the time yourself.

4.2 · Where MICS would then improve on his setup

  • One clock instead of five. Every digital event on the rig timestamped in microseconds, on one timebase — against a ~1 ms software-timed digital input today.
  • The weeks before the rig become data. Body weight against a baseline, surgeries, projects, protocols, per animal, across months — instead of a spreadsheet. For a lab whose independent variable is deprivation state, this is the least instrumented part of the experiment.
  • The task is stored, checkable and changeable without code. Content-hashed, statically analysable — including a deadlock check that catches tasks that can never finish — and editable in a GUI. His current task script is not saved in his data at all.
  • Ask questions across animals and days, live. Instead of opening 200 files one at a time — with dashboards anyone in the lab can open from their own desk while a session runs, rather than a panel on the rig's own screen.
  • Many rigs, unattended. One animal per PC becomes many rigs from one place, running overnight.
  • Everything else he owns can plug in and land on the same clock — camera, tracker, ephys.

Section 06Questions to expect — and how to answer

"My science is about need states. How would this actually help me?"
Answer narrowly — he trains in MonkeyLogic too, so do not offer to train his mice. Offer the layer his rig has no concept of: weight curves against a baseline, surgery records, projects, protocols, and the ability to ask questions across animals and days. Then ask how his lab tracks restriction today rather than assuming it is a spreadsheet.
"Can you show my visual stimulus?"
Not today — and it is the hardest case, because a drifting grating must be correct on every screen refresh. But the answer is "here is what it would take", not "we can't": a grating implementation with the right parameters already exists in the codebase, unused, already on its own thread and already waiting for the screen's beat.
"Do you handle analog signals?"
No — it is the one capability MonkeyLogic has that we have no version of. Then show the plan in three beats: an ADC board; a driver that publishes derived values so a non-programmer can use them; and batching, because one record per sample at 1 kHz would dwarf the entire existing database. Then turn it round — ask which signals he genuinely needs analog for, since the frame clock, capacitive licking and a running wheel are all digital.
"Can you sync to my two-photon frame clock?"
Yes in principle — the pulse hardware exists — but the wiring in the live toolkit is currently switched off, so it is one config change plus a demo run, and it has not been demonstrated. Do not bluff; he will ask for the jitter number and there isn't one yet.
"What is your lick-to-reward latency?"
It has never been measured. The only instrumented number is a ~5 ms mean, 46 ms worst-case loop measured on an LED, before the latency fixes and never repeated. Say exactly that, then say what you would measure and how. That lands better than a number he cannot check.
"How do you record a trial?"
Trial boundaries, hits, misses and false alarms are all logged as events, and every state the task passes through is logged too — so the whole route through a trial is recoverable, which is richer than a single outcome code. What is missing is the guarantee: consistent names and shapes, and a trial number on every record.
"Can I do closed-loop stimulation based on neural activity?"
Not today. Be careful here — MonkeyLogic has shipped a working closed-loop stimulation example since 2017, and can take a neural signal in as an analog voltage or a live stream. Claiming otherwise would be wrong. Note that neither system does two-photon holographic closed loop; that lives in the microscope software.
"How many animals per computer?"
Theirs: exactly one, structurally, in the maintainer's own words. Ours: many rigs from one place, with device locking and a web interface. This is the strongest structural contrast in the talk — state their number from their own documentation, not from an assumption.
"Who maintains it, and what happens when the student leaves?"
The fairest reciprocal question, and it cuts both ways. Answer honestly about our bus factor, then point at what reduces it: tasks stored as data rather than code, drivers as versioned records, and automated checking.
Two arguments that do NOT survive checking — avoid both

1. "MATLAB licensing makes it expensive to scale." False in an academic setting — about $550 a seat, often zero under a campus agreement, and the DAQ board costs four times more.

2. "MonkeyLogic can't do closed loop." False — it has shipped a working example since November 2017.

The defensible structural claims are: one animal per PC · one screen frame of loop granularity · no data layer above the file · no unattended operation · several clocks.

Confidence. Claims about MonkeyLogic come from its own documentation, source distribution, maintainer forum posts and published papers. Claims about the Livneh lab come from their published Methods. Claims about MICS come from its source and a live query of the event database. Numbers with no source stated are not on this page.
