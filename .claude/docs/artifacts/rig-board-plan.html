01 — Bottom line

Five things to take away

A
Only four pins on this header are genuinely special.
The two I2C lines, TOUCH_INT, and a TTL sync out. Everything else — every valve, every LED, every beam-break — is a generic line that can sit anywhere, or move off the header entirely.
B
One question decides where a generic line lives.
Does this edge’s timestamp appear in your analysis? Yes → it needs a real GPIO so pigpio can DMA-sample the edge. No → it belongs on an I2C expander, which costs zero header pins and gives you sixteen at a time.
C
A real DAC needs three pins, not four, and the Pi becomes the audio clock.
A bare PCM5102A wants BCK, LRCK and DOUT — no I2C, no HAT EEPROM. As I2S master the Pi generates the sample clock from the same crystal as the pigpio tick. USB cannot give you that; only a sync channel buys it back.
D
The audio latency is a bug in mixer.py, and you have never measured onset.
Every trigger re-decodes the WAV on the calling thread, which is why preloading to RAM changed nothing. And the event is timestamped when play() returns — before a single sample leaves the buffer.
E
Never “fix the format” of PIGPIOMASK.
Correcting the stock binary string to its literal hex would disable four generic outputs with no error the pilot surfaces. Found on the rig; now set to 0x0FFFFFFC. See section 07.

The classification rule, in one question: does this line’s edge time appear in your analysis? If yes it belongs on the header, because only a real GPIO edge gets DMA-sampled onto the pigpio tick. If no, it belongs on an expander. Nothing else about the signal matters — not what it drives, not what it is called. Note that this is a scientific judgement, not an engineering one: an air puff is soft-timed in one protocol and a hard-timed unconditioned stimulus in the next.

Against today’s rig: 22 generic lines in use — 15 out, 7 in — and one of those retires when the OptoBlueBerry trigger moves to BLE, so 21 are genuinely needed. Fit the DAC and the header offers 21. It fits exactly.

Add the audio sync return and you are one short, so a single soft-timed output moves to an expander. Move five instead — the odour bank is the obvious candidate, since odour arrival is diffusion-limited at tens to hundreds of milliseconds — and you also recover the whole SPI bus, because those five happen to sit on it. That is only worth doing if you need kHz analog.

That is the entire design problem, and it has nothing to do with which valve is which.

04 — The three routes

Three pins, sound only

Relocate three generic outputs onto the two spare pins and the one the BlueHub trigger vacates. Drop a PCM5102A on the I2S lines.

05 — The audio bug

Why preloading to RAM changed nothing

Imagine a DJ who has to press the vinyl before every drop.

You want a beep the instant the mouse licks. Your code’s job at that moment should be one thing: hit play. Instead, on every single trigger, it goes back to the record shop, buys a blank, cuts the groove, and only then puts the needle down.

That is literally what mixer.Sound(path) does inside the trigger call. It opens the file, decodes it, allocates memory for the samples, and resamples 192 kHz down to the 44.1 kHz that SDL quietly opened the device at — because mixer.init(buffer=1024) was never told a frequency.

So when you preloaded the files into RAM, you moved the record shop next door. Helpful, if the walk had been the problem. It wasn’t. The slow part was pressing the vinyl, and you still do that every time. Worse: how long it takes depends on how big the file is and what mood the memory allocator is in — which is exactly the random, won’t-go-away jitter you described.

autopilot/hardware/mixer.py def __init__(self, **kwargs): mixer.init(buffer=1024) # no frequency= → SDL opens at 44100 Hz self.sound_list = [...] # only FILENAMES are cached, not audio @log_action def set(self, sound_file_index, volume=0.5): sound = mixer.Sound(...) # ← open + decode + resample, EVERY trigger mixer.Channel(0).play(sound) # the timestamp is taken when THIS returns

Then the second half: the clock you write down is the wrong one.

Hitting play does not make sound. It puts samples in a queue. At 1024 frames and 44.1 kHz that queue is 23.2 milliseconds long, and ALSA_NPERIODS=3 sits behind it.

But @log_action stamps the event the moment play() returns — before a single sample has left the buffer. So the number in your data includes all of the variable decode cost and none of the fixed buffer delay. It is wrong in one direction by an unknown amount and in the other by a constant you have never measured.

That is the real finding. You have been trying to remove jitter from a number that was never measuring the thing you cared about. Fix the decode, fix the buffer — then wire one DAC channel back into a GPIO input through a comparator, let pigpio’s DMA sampler timestamp its edge, and for the first time the event log will contain the moment the speaker actually moved.

06 — Audio vs analog

Two subsystems that share nothing

These keep getting conflated. Plainly: audio is a fast AC waveform on three dedicated pins. Analog is a slow DC level on the I2C bus you already have. Different chips, different buses, different wiring, different clocks. Adding one does not get you the other.

Why everything is a breakout

A HAT covers the whole header and carries an ID EEPROM on BCM0/BCM1 — the two spare pins this plan depends on. Check with ls /proc/device-tree/hat/: absent or empty means no EEPROM, and the pins are genuinely free.

Mandatory

A tachogenerator’s output rises with speed and swings negative on reversal; the ADS1115 tolerates ±6.144 V and never more than VDD+0.3 V. Spin the belt at full speed, read the open-circuit voltage, size the divider from that, and feed it differentially.

07 — I2C bus design

Design the bus before anything is soldered

I2C is the one subsystem where a casual decision now is expensive later: the address space and the pull-up network are properties of the whole bus, not of any one board. You cannot fix them a board at a time.

Measured on RecordingBox, 7 Sep 2026. i2cdetect -y 1 returns exactly one device: 0x5a. One MPR121, nothing else. And i2cdetect -l shows only i2c-1 is usable — i2c-20/i2c-21 are the HDMI DDC buses. So what look like two I2C buses are two sockets daisy-chained on one bus: same wires, same address space, same bandwidth.

Reserve the whole address space, once

0x20–0x27MCP23017 — 16 extra on/off pins over I2C, for slow switches and LEDs8

0x60 is the only contested address. Settle it now and write it down: the DAC keeps 0x60, any PCA9685 starts at 0x61 — one solder jumper on the shield. A PCA9685 also answers on its all-call address 0x70; that is the same chip appearing twice in a scan, not a second device.

The pin cost scales with IRQ lines, not chips

An MPR121 costs nothing on the bus — it is address-selected. But each one needs its own GPIO for its IRQ, and that is the part that eats the budget.

Do not wire-OR two IRQs onto one pin to save it. i2c.py already fights a level-held latch on a single chip; two chips sharing a line makes a missed deassertion far harder to reason about and to recover from. The generic pin count drops from 20 to 19 with a second lick chip — twelve electrodes is already a lot of lickers, so decide before the board is laid out.

Gotcha

Pull-ups stack, and that is what kills long buses

The Pi has fixed 1.8 kΩ pull-ups on BCM2/3. Nearly every breakout adds its own 10 kΩ. Five in parallel gives ~1.5 kΩ, past the 3 mA sink limit — the bus stops reaching a valid low and you get intermittent errors that look like anything but a resistor. De-populate the pull-ups on every board except the one at the far end.

Sockets

Identical and interchangeable

SDA, SCL, 3V3, GND, plus a dedicated IRQ line back to its own GPIO. Then any socket takes an MPR121 or an analog board with no rework. Reserve IRQ pins for two sockets; DACs, ADCs and expanders need none.

Length

Under a metre at 400 kHz

The spec limit is 400 pF of bus capacitance and ribbon cable eats it quickly. Twisted pair with a ground return. Past that, add a buffer such as a TCA9517.

Speed

Set it explicitly — the default is 100 kHz

dtparam=i2c_arm_baudrate=400000. At 100 kHz an ADS1115 read costs ~400 µs, so continuous sampling at 860 SPS burns ~34% of the bus; at 400 kHz it is ~9%. Test once after changing it — a daisy-chain of sockets is exactly the geometry that makes 400 kHz marginal.

One bus or two?

One. Enabling i2c3–i2c6 costs 2 GPIO each and the budget has none to give. The case for a second bus is isolating lick reads from bulk ADC traffic — but MPR121 reads are IRQ-driven and rare, and an ADS1115 running flat out at 400 kHz uses about 9% of the bus. Not worth two pins.

What faster I2C does not fix: lick timing. The TOUCH_INT edge is DMA-timestamped by pigpio before any I2C happens; the bus read only reports which electrode. Speed buys quicker closed-loop response and room for the ADC, not better lick timestamps.

One live mismatch worth resolving. prefs.json declares MOTOR_SHIELD_HAT, DOOR1, DOOR2 and MOTORIZED_REWARD at 0x60, and nothing answers there. A missing motor HAT fails silently, so a task definition pulling in those modules on this box would give no error and no motion. Either the shield belongs on RecordingBox, or those entries are stale copy from the door-test rig.
08 — Assembly

Building the box

Plan B, drawn as it would be built. Everything that switches current lives on its own rail with its own return; everything that leaves the box crosses an isolator.

Wiring

Two rails, one ground point

Valve switching sags a shared 5 V rail — that lands as an audible click on the DAC and as noise on the analog out. Separate supplies, flyback diodes at every solenoid, star ground.

Panel

BNC is not 50 Ω here

Pi GPIO is 3.3 V CMOS. Buffer every output, series-resist and ESD-clamp every input, and opto-isolate both directions — BNC shells create ground loops the moment a second instrument joins.

Breakout

Label by class, wire by class

Bring the generic lines to identical, numbered panel positions rather than to “VALVE1” and “ODOR3”. A rig that is rewired for a new protocol should need no new panel, and no relabelling.

Expander

Keep one bank soft

Reserve a contiguous block of panel positions for the expander outputs and mark them as such. They are the pins you can always add more of; the header ones are not.

Mechanical

SD slot, not an extender

The Pi 4B card is a friction fit with no eject spring. Align a slot in the wall with the board edge and leave finger clearance. Extender ribbons corrupt cards at speed — don’t.

Layout

Keep RFID away from the lickers

134.2 kHz excitation couples straight into MPR121 electrodes. Shield and separate the antenna from the electrode leads, or duty-cycle the reader so it never energises inside a lick window.

What plugs in, what gets soldered, what gets built

Plugs in

MCP4728 and ADS1115

Straight into the free I2C socket. If socket and breakout share a 4-pin JST/Qwiic it is a cable and nothing else; otherwise a 4-wire pigtail. The one fiddly part: de-populate the onboard pull-ups on all but one board.

Soldered

PCM5102A only

Five wires to the header — 3V3, GND, BCK→pin 12, LRCK→pin 35, DIN→pin 40 — plus the two config connections that fail silently if skipped: XSMT pulled high, SCK tied to GND. Output pads to the amplifier.

Built

The analog conditioning

Treadmill in: divider + clamp + series resistor into a differential input. Light onset: GPIO → buffer/opto → the driver’s TTL gate. Analog out: possibly nothing — see the check below. Half a day of veroboard, or one small PCB.

Check first

Your LED driver’s input spec

The MCP4728 has a rail-to-rail buffer and drives ~1 kΩ directly. If the driver takes 0–3.3 V into a high-impedance input, wire it straight — no conditioning at all. If it wants 0–5 V you lose a third of the range; a ×1.5 op-amp stage recovers it.

Don’t

Power the DAC from 5 V to get 5 V out

The MCP4728’s I2C logic-high threshold is 0.7 × VDD = 3.5 V at a 5 V supply, and the Pi drives only 3.3 V. It would work intermittently and be miserable to diagnose. Run it at 3.3 V and add the gain stage.

Pattern

The DAC sets how bright, a GPIO sets when

A DAC write is software-timestamped, ~150 µs at 400 kHz — fine for a level, not a hardware edge. Set intensity during the ITI, gate the light with a TTL at onset. That is why commercial optogenetic drivers have separate analog and TTL inputs.

09 — Doors and motors

They cost an address, not a pin

No, doors do not need another bus. The Adafruit motor HAT is a PCA9685 — a 16-channel PWM generator — driving TB6612FNG H-bridges, the power stage that actually turns a DC motor. It talks I2C at 0x60 on the bus you already have, and prefs.json runs three motors through it: DOOR1, DOOR2 and MOTORIZED_REWARD. Header cost: zero.

But the HAT form factor fights the audio plan

Two problems, and the second is decisive. It covers the 40-pin header, so the audio DAC’s five soldered wires become a pass-through exercise. And if its ID EEPROM is populated it claims BCM0/BCM1 — exactly the two spare pins the audio DAC depends on. That would break Plan A outright.

Still outstanding: ls /proc/device-tree/hat/. Absent or empty means no EEPROM was detected at boot and the pins are genuinely free.

For the new rig: use the chips, not the shield

A motor HAT is not a special device — it is two ordinary chips in a shield-shaped package. Buy them as breakouts and put them on the carrier board instead.

Headroom

One PCA9685 drives up to 8 motors through 4 H-bridges — well past the three in use. If the doors are servos rather than DC motors, the PCA9685 alone is enough; servos take a PWM pulse directly and need no H-bridge.

Fix

Motors are being left stalled

A recorded defect. Stalling a DC motor against an end stop draws locked-rotor current indefinitely — it cooks the motor and the driver, and fails months later. Add two limit switches per door so it stops on arrival.

Where the limit switches go. They are not timing-critical, so they belong on an MCP23017 — its interrupt-on-change gives you all 16 inputs for one GPIO pin. That is the cheapest available fix for a real hardware-damage risk. The other recorded defect, a missing motor shield failing silently, is separate and lives in software: instantiating a motor module should fail loudly when nothing answers at its address.

Scaling

Addresses are nowhere near the limit — a realistic rig is five devices against a 112-address space. Bandwidth is not the limit either: at 400 kHz an ADS1115 running flat out is ~8.6% of the bus, and everything else writes only on change. The real ceiling is physical — the 400 pF capacitance limit and the stacking pull-ups. Fifteen devices down a metre of ribbon is trouble; the fix is a TCA9517 bus buffer, not a second bus.

Motors scale by adding boards, not buses or pins. One PCA9685 has 16 PWM channels and a DC motor eats 3 of them — speed plus two direction lines.

Scales in pins

Limit switches, and only those

An MCP23017 gives 16 inputs, so 8 doors per chip, each wanting one GPIO for its interrupt. Past that you may wire-OR two interrupt outputs onto one pin — safe here, unlike the MPR121, because the MCP23017’s interrupt clears on an ordinary read and a missed limit edge is recoverable by a poll.

Power

Size for stall, not running current

A DC motor at its end stop draws several times its running draw, and a sequence commanding several doors stalls them together. TB6612FNG is 1.2 A continuous / 3.2 A peak per channel — four doors is ~2 A on a rail that must stay separate from the logic supply.

Can the Pi replace the BlueHub? The control unit, yes. Pi 4B is BT 5.0 / BLE and speaks to the RN4871 through bleak. This frees one generic output — the third pin the DAC needs. Is Bluetooth even on? no rfkill reports hci0 soft-blocked and hciconfig reports DOWN on RecordingBox today. WiFi too. Unblocking puts a 2.4 GHz transmitter inside the recording box. On an Ethernet-wired rig that block is often deliberate — a decision, not a command.
Does Bluetooth cost a header pin? no pinctrl confirms the controller is on the internal PL011 — GPIO30–33, in alt3. Nothing. This closes an open question from the first draft.
How many BlueBerries at once? 7–10 concurrent BLE links on a CYW43455. Run one connection-manager process — multi-process bleak aborts connections on a Pi. Onboard BT shares the antenna with 2.4 GHz WiFi. Put rigs on Ethernet or 5 GHz.
Does RFID cost GPIO? no FDX-B 134.2 kHz readers go to a powered USB hub, one per zone. The header UART is unavailable anyway — two of its pins are already generic I/O. Arrival times are userspace, not pigpio ticks. Log them as software-timestamped and say so.
Use the MPR121 as an expander? no ELE4–11 can be GPIO, but i2c.py deliberately manipulates the ECR register to keep the level-held TOUCH_INT line recoverable. Adding writes to that register space invites exactly the latched-IRQ failure the code was hardened against. Use a dedicated MCP23017.
11 — Timestamping

Getting I2C events onto the one counter

An I2C transaction cannot be timestamped on the pigpio tick — @log_action stamps it ts_source="software" when the call returns. But almost every I2C chip has a spare pin that can witness the event on a real GPIO, where pigpio DMA-samples the edge to microseconds.

Inputs are timestamped by their interrupt or ready line. Outputs are timestamped by a GPIO that gates them.

The rig already does this without naming it: the MPR121’s IRQ on BCM14 says when a lick happened; the I2C read afterwards says which electrode. Edge = when, bus = what.
Witness pinChip pinWhat it timestamps

Doors

The limit switch is the timestamp

The I2C command was never the door’s arrival time — mechanical travel sits in between. Command = when we asked; switch edge = when it arrived; the difference is measured travel time per trial. One pin covers any number of doors: wire-OR every switch for the edge, read the expander to learn which closed.

Treadmill

Anchor and interpolate

Configure ALERT/RDY to pulse per conversion (Hi_thresh MSB 1, Lo_thresh MSB 0). Timestamping all 860/s is heavy and unnecessary — take one anchor every Nth conversion and interpolate between. The anchors also measure the ADC oscillator drifting against the tick, free.

Light

Use LDAC, one pin does both jobs

Write values over I2C whenever; pulse LDAC from a GPIO and all four channels update at that instant. The pin both causes and timestamps the change. Specify the MCP4728 over the MCP4725 even for one channel — the 4725 has no LDAC and can never be hardware-timestamped.

Cannot

The PCA9685 has no witness output

A motor command cannot self-witness, which is exactly why the limit switch matters. Same for any output whose chip offers no ready or latch pin — gate it with a GPIO or accept a software stamp.

What RecordingBox actually says

Read-only probes against 132.77.73.213 on 7 Sep 2026. Pi 4B Rev 1.5, Debian 13 trixie, kernel 6.18.39, /opt/mics at 43f7b7b, 52 °C, not throttled.

Two corrections to the first draft. BCM27 sitting as an output is not a fault — clock_soak.py takes --out-pin/--in-pin and nothing restores pin modes on exit, so BCM26/27 are soak residue. And dtoverlay=nospi10 is a real stock overlay whose help reads “Disable the spi10 device on Pi5” — on a BCM2711 it does nothing at all.

The landmine: PIGPIOMASK

pigpiod parses -x as a number, not as bits:

case 'x': mask = getNum(optarg, &err); // strtol family, base 0 if (!err) { updateMask = mask; updateMaskSet = 1; } else fatal("invalid -x option (%s)", optarg);

The shipped value 1111110000111111111111110000 has no 0x prefix, so it is read as decimal — about 1.1×1027, which overflows a 64-bit integer. pigpiod is running, so it never hit fatal(); it simply took whatever the overflow produced. The mask has never done anything, which is exactly what you saw when changing it on the old OS.

Now read it as bits. MSB = GPIO27, it means 0x0FC3FFF0: exclude GPIO0–3, and exclude GPIO18, 19, 20, 21 — four generic outputs, one of which is also an I2S line. And it was never a MICS decision: the string is Autopilot’s stock default, verbatim from prefs.py and repeated in pilot.py’s docstring. It has ridden along inert since the fork.

So the danger is not the broken mask. It is that the mask looks deliberate. The day someone tidies it to -x 0x0FC3FFF0, four outputs stop responding — silently, because pigpio just declines the mode change.

Fixed

Mask is now 0x0FFFFFFC

BCM2–27. Changed in prefs.template.json, in prefs.py’s default so a fresh prefs cannot regenerate it, and in the docstring example.

Pinned

test_pigpio_mask.py

Asserts the mask parses as a number and permits every BCM pin the HARDWARE dict declares. All four assertions failed against the old value.

Deploy

prefs.json is untracked

A git pull does not update the live file. Edit /opt/mics/pilot/prefs.json by hand and restart the pilot — start_pigpiod() reads it at launch.

Watch

The two spare pins stay outside

Both the new value and pigpiod’s own default stop at BCM2. Any plan that lands hardware on BCM0/BCM1 must widen it to 0x0FFFFFFF at the same time.

What the pin captures could and could not settle

Two pinctrl get captures — one idle, one during a clockSoak run — are identical: every GPIO 4–25 an input at its power-on pull. That is not evidence about the pin map. The running task’s hardware set held only the Modules entries from the backend, so init_hardware() did run but the set has no GPIO, no I2C and no Mixer members. Nothing ever asked pigpio for an output, and nothing opened the audio device.

Still outstanding: one pinctrl get plus an hw_params capture during a task whose toolkit includes GPIO hardware and AUDIO1. That is the only measurement that shows the real pin state and the rate, format and period size the device actually runs at.

13 — Before anyone orders parts

Answer these in order

The first two are decisions. The rest are bench work, and none of them needs a purchase order.

  1. Which generic lines are hard-timed?Go through the rig one line at a time and ask whether its edge timestamp appears in your analysis. That count, not the valve names, is what the header has to hold. Everything else moves to an expander.
  2. Is 7.5–30 ms of BLE onset jitter acceptable for your stimulation protocols?If yes, the BlueHub trigger line is free and the DAC fits. If no, that line stays wired and the third pin has to come off an expander instead.
  3. Build the sync-channel fixture on the current rig, before buying anything.It turns “we couldn’t get rid of the latency” into a measured distribution — and will almost certainly show the decode-per-trigger bug accounts for most of it, which is a free fix.
  4. Bench-test a 134.2 kHz reader against the MPR121 electrodes.If they interfere, RFID moves to HF 13.56 MHz and the mechanical layout changes with it. Cheaper to find out before the box is cut.
  5. Measure the treadmill’s open-circuit voltage at full speed.Everything about the input divider and clamp follows from that one number, and the ADS1115 has hard absolute limits. Two minutes with a multimeter.
  6. Decide whether the motor shield belongs on this rig at all.prefs.json declares it and three door/reward modules at 0x60; nothing answers there, and a missing HAT fails silently. Either fit it — and move it to 0x61 — or delete the stale entries.
  7. Set dtparam=i2c_arm_baudrate=400000.You are on the 100 kHz default, where a continuously sampling ADS1115 would take a third of the bus. Test the daisy-chain once after changing it.
  8. Decide whether Bluetooth may be on inside the recording box.Soft-blocked today. Gates the whole OptoBlueBerry section, and it is an RF-hygiene call rather than a technical one.
  9. Never “fix the format” of PIGPIOMASK.Correcting it to its literal hex would disable four generic outputs. It is now 0x0FFFFFFC; widen to 0x0FFFFFFF the day anything lands on the two spare pins.
